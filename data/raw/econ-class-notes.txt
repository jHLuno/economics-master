Supply (Qs) - the quantity of a product or service that producers are willing and
able to sell at a given price over a specific time period.
Law of supply - as the price of a product rises, the quantity supplied of the
product will usually increase, ceteris paribus.
Higher prices = greater potential profit → more incentive to produce.
Lower prices = less profit → producers reduce supply.

Factors affecting supply (shifters) - a supply shifter is any non-price factor that
changes how much producers are willing and able to sell at every price. When a
supply shifter changes, the entire supply curve shifts (left or right).

●

●

More sellers - supply shifts right. Fewer sellers - supply shifts left.

●

Taxes/Subsidies - Policies change firms’ costs and incentives. Tax on
producers = supply shifts left. Subsidy to producers = supply shifts
right.

●

Expectations - firms may change today’s supply based on what they
expect tomorrow. Expect higher future price - supply today can fall
(hold inventory). Expect higher future costs - supply today can rise
(produce more now).

Utility - the satisfaction or happiness derived from consuming a good/service.
Rational consumers aim to maximize their utility given their budget constraints.
Total utility (TU) - total satisfaction or happiness a person gets from consuming
a certain amount of goods or services. Think of it as the sum total of joy or
benefit you derive from all the units of a product or service you consume.
Marginal utility (MU) - the additional satisfaction gained from consuming one
more unit of a good/service. As consumption increases, marginal utility typically
diminishes.

INDIFFERENCE CURVES
An indifference curve shows different combinations of two
goods that provide the consumer with the same level of satisfaction or utility.

The marginal rate of substitution (MRS) is the quantity of one good that a
consumer can forego for additional units of another good at the same utility
level.
MRS = - MU(x) / MU(y)

MRS = const (For goods that are perfect substitutes, the indifference curves
are straight lines with a constant slope, indicating that the consumer views the
goods as equally replaceable)
MRS = 0 (For highly complementing goods, the indifference curve takes a sharp
right angle shape (L-shaped). This happens because you need a fixed ratio of
both goods to derive any satisfaction)

The marginal product of an input is the additional output produced by adding
one more unit of that input, holding all other inputs constant.
After a certain point, adding more of the input leads to a smaller and smaller
increase in output. This occurs because the additional input becomes less
effective as it is over-utilized relative to the fixed inputs.

Tacit
Circumstance where two companies agree upon a certain strategy without
putting it in writing or spelling out the strategy explicitly.

The national economy as a system.
Economics - Is a social science that studies how individuals, firms, and
societies make choices to allocate limited resources to satisfy their unlimited
wants.
Microeconomics is a branch of economics that analyzes market behavior of
individuals and firms in order to understand their decision-making process.
Macroeconomics is a branch of economics that studies how an overall economy
- the markets, businesses, consumers and governments behave.
National Economy - the system of production, distribution and trade,
consumption of goods and services by different agents of a nation.
The primary aim of this system is to allocate limited resources efficiently to
meet the unlimited wants and needs of its population.
Other goals: 1) Improving efficiency; 2) Equity; 3) Economic freedom; 4)
Economic growth; 5) Full employment; 6) Price stability
Key components of the national economy:

. Households - the consumers of goods and services, who also offer
labor and other resources to the production process.
. Business - entities that produce goods and services. They use labor,
capital and natural resources to create outputs.
. Government - plays a dual role by being a consumer and a provider. It
consumes resources to provide public goods and services and
regulates economic activities through policies and laws.

. The foreign sector - represents trade and financial transactions with
other countries.
Key economic indicators:

. GDP - total market value of all final goods and services produced
within a country in a given period.
. Unemployment rate - the percentage of the labor force that is jobless
and actively seeking employment.
. Inflation Rate - the rate at which the general level of prices for goods
and services is rising.
Economic Systems are organized ways in which societies determine the
ownership, direction, allocation, and distribution of economic resources.
Traditional Economic System - based on custom, beliefs, and traditions.
Economic roles and decisions are defined by cultural practices passed down
through generations (Pros: stability and predictability. Cons: resistance to
change and innovation).
Command (Planned Economy) System - the government or a central authority
makes all decisions regarding the production and distribution of goods and
services, ownership of resources is state-controlled (Pros: rapidly mobilize
resources on a large scale. Cons: lack of incentives for efficiency and
innovation).
Market Economic System - decisions regarding investment, production, and
distribution are based on market conditions, such as supply and demand,
private individuals and businesses own most resources (Pros: encourages
innovation and efficiency through competition. Cons: lead to income inequality
and market failures where unregulated markets fail to produce efficiently or
equitably).
Mixed Economic System - combines elements of market, command, and
sometimes traditional systems. Both the government and private sector play
significant roles in economic decision-making. (Pros: Balances efficiency and
innovation of the market with ability to address inequalities and provide public
goods. Cons: the balance between government intervention and market
freedom can be difficult to achieve and maintain).
Transition Economies - transitioning from a command to a market-based
system. These economies are undergoing significant structural reforms to move
towards more market-oriented models. (Pros: potential for rapid economic
growth and development as market efficiencies are introduced. Cons: transition
can be marked by economic instability, uncertainty and disparities in wealth and
income.

Macroeconomics indicators - statistics or readings that reflect the economic
circumstances of a particular country, region or sector. They are used by
analysts and governments to assess the current and future health of the
economy and financial markets.
Types of Macroeconomics Indicators: 1) Leading Indicators; 2) Lagging
Indicators; 3) Coincident Indicators
Leading Indicators - these predict future economic activity. Yield curve,
consumer durables, net business formations, and share prices.
Lagging Indicators - these data sets show information after events have
happened. GNP, CPI, unemployment rates, and interest rates.
Coincident Indicators - these change at approximately the same time as the
economy as a whole, providing information about the current state of the
economy. GDP, employment levels, and retail sales.
Pros and cons:
●

May accurately forecast what is to come based on prevailing data
● Often use publicly available information
● May be calculated using the same process over and over, particularly
when issued by governments
● May be released on a fixed, predictable cadence
– May not accurately predict the future
– Rely on many assumptions, some of which may be unpredictable
– Can be open for interpretation
– Still require expertise to interpret and understand

Economic growth refers to the increase in the inflation-adjusted market value of
the goods and services produced by an economy over time. This is often
measured by the rate of change in a country’s GDP.
Important because leads to increased incomes, higher standards of living and a
reduction in poverty.
Extensive economic growth - occurs when an economy grows by increasing the
quantity of inputs used in production, such as labor, capital, and natural
resources. Utilizing more resources expands the scale of economic activity.
Intensive economic growth - occurs when an economy grows by increasing the
productivity of its existing resources. Improve in efficiency, technology and the
quality leads to growth.
Productivity - the amount of goods and services produced for each hour of a
worker’s time.
Y = A * f(L, K, H, N)
L - number of workers; K - stock of physical capital; H - stock of human capital;
N - stock of natural resources; A - stock of technological knowledge.

Money and the banking system
Money is a commodity accepted by general consent as a medium of economic
exchange.
Functions:

. A medium of exchange - money facilitates the exchange of goods and
services by eliminating the need for barter.
. A unit of account - money provides a common measure of the value of
goods and services, enabling comparison and calculation of prices and
costs.
. A store of value - money allows individuals to store purchasing power
for future use, preserving wealth over time.
Intrinsic value: Yes (Commodity Money) - it has a value apart from its use as
money, may vary in quality, the system has costs of production, storage and
transportation of the commodity
Intrinsic value: No (Fiat Money) - legal tender, its not linked to a tangible asset,
can be regulated with greater flexibility.
Basic principle - What makes something money is really found in its
acceptability, not in whether or not it has intrinsic value or whether or not a
government has declared it as such.
Liquidity - The ease with which an asset, or security, can be converted into
ready cash without affecting its market price. Assets that are considered highly
liquid can be quickly converted into cash with minimal impact on their market
value.

Money Demand (MD) - the demand for money refers to the desire to hold
money (cash or in bank accounts) instead of investing it or spending it
immediately.

. Transaction Demand - the need for money for day-to-day
transactions.
. Precautionary Demand - the need for money for unexpected expenses.
. Speculative Demand - the need for money to take advantage of
investment opportunities.
Income Level high -> Money Demand High
Interest Rates low -> Money Demand High
Price Level high -> Money Demand High
Economic Uncertainty -> Money Demand High
Money Supply (MS) - the total amount of money: cash, coins and balances in
bank accounts - in circulation.
M1 Money Supply - called “narrow money” includes M0 (cash) and other highly
liquid deposits in the bank
M2 Money Supply - M1 + marketable securities and less liquid deposits
M3 Money Supply - M2 + mutual funds, repurchase agreements, commercial
papers.
M4 Money Supply - M3 + other least liquid assets, usually outside commercial
banks.
Equilibrium in financial markets requires that money supply (MS) be equal to
money demand (MD).

Velocity of Money is a measurement of the rate at which money is exchanged in

Identifying underlying issues - a change in the velocity of money can be a

M*V=P*T
M - money supply; V - velocity of money; P - average price level; T - volume of

MONEY CREATION (ISSUANCE)

Money creation primarily occurs in two ways: through central banking
operations and through the commercial banking system.
Open market operations (OMO), the central bank’s primary tool for regulating
money supply, involve buying or selling government bonds to increase or
decrease the money in circulation.
Quantitative Easing (QE) is a large-scale monetary policy used during severe
economic downturns. The central bank buys longer-term securities to inject
money into the financial system, aiming to lower interest rates and stimulate
lending and investment.
Issuing Currency: The central bank also has the exclusive right to issue
currency, which directly increases the money supply when more currency is put
into circulation.
Fractional-reserve banking allows banks to create new money by lending out a
fraction of deposits. Deposits and loans create a multiplicative effect on money
creation, with a $100 deposit potentially generating $900 in loans and $1,000 in
new money.
Structure of the Banking System:

. Central Bank - Central banks regulate monetary and banking policies,
issue currency, manage reserves, and set interest rates.
. Commercial Banks - Retail banks provide everyday banking services
like deposits, loans, and accounts.
. Investment Banks - These banks serve businesses, governments, and
institutional investors, helping raise capital through underwriting and
securities issuance.
. Savings and Loan Associations - These institutions traditionally
specialized in savings accounts and real estate financing.
. Credit Unions - Member-owned, not-for-profit organizations provide
banking services.
Banks facilitate the flow of funds, provide payment systems, offer financial
services, manage risk, and create credit, all of which are essential for economic
growth. Banks influence economic growth and inflation by controlling money
supply and lending rates. Banks provide liquidity to meet short-term needs.
Banks assess credit risk and allocate resources to minimize financial crises.

The Gold Standard was a monetary system where currency was tied to gold,
with countries converting paper money to gold upon demand. The Classical
Gold Standard (1870-1914) facilitated international trade and investment with
fixed exchange rates and low inflation. However, it limited monetary policy due
to reliance on gold reserves.

After WWI, countries struggled to restore the gold standard due to uneven gold
reserves and inflation. The Great Depression worsened as the gold standard
limited monetary policy, leading to deflation and economic stagnation.
The Bretton Woods Agreement established a new global monetary system,
pegging the USD to gold and other currencies to the dollar. However, excessive
dollar printing without corresponding gold reserves led to a loss of confidence
and the eventual abandonment of the gold standard in 1971.

Credit and Banking System
Credit is the ability of a borrower to obtain goods, services, or money in the
present in exchange for a promise to repay in the future, usually with interest.
The principal (основная сумма) is the original amount of money that is
borrowed or lent, before interest is added.
Interest is the cost of borrowing money, typically expressed as an annual
percentage of the principal, called the interest rate.
Interest = P(principal) * r(annual interest rate) * t (time in years)
Compound Interest is calculated on the initial principal and also on the
accumulated interest from previous periods - “interest on interest”.
A = P ( 1 + r/n)^nt
A = final amount; P = principal; r = annual rate; n = number of compounding
periods per year; t = time in years.
